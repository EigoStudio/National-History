第一章 原始：2. 縄文時代

（イ）日本の縄文土器は世界最古級p.26-27

🎧 Listen to the Audio
【課題】日本列島ではいつから土器が使われたのだろう。 日本では、土器が作られるようになったのを縄文時代の始まり、また、本格的な水田稲作の開始を弥生時代の始まり（縄文時代の終わり）と定義しています。 　平成十年（一九九八）、青森県外ヶ浜町の民家の建て替え工事にあたり、旧蟹田町教育委員会が行った発掘調査で発見された土器が、世界の考古学の歴史を大きく塗り替えることになりました。 　発見された土器は、すべて小さな破片で、模様はありませんでした。付着していた炭化物などを試料に行った放射性炭素年代測定法による検査により、出土した土器は、約一万六三〇〇年前の世界最古級の土器であることが分かったのです。この測定の結果、日本における土器の出現がこれまで考えられていた時期より約四〇〇〇年遡りました。その後、日本列島の広い範囲で、古い時代の土器が多数確認されました。たとえば、長崎県の福井洞窟の土器は約一万六〇〇〇年前、東京都の御殿山遺跡の土器は約一万六三〇〇年前、神奈川県の宮ケ瀬北原の土器は約一万五五〇〇年前、北海道の大正3遺跡の土器は約一万四五〇〇年前と見られています。福井洞窟と御殿山遺跡は、外ヶ浜町の遺跡とほとんど同時期であり、短期間のうちに北は北海道、西は九州にまで伝播したことが分かります。 　この遺跡から出土した石鏃（石でできた矢尻）は世界で最古のものと見られています。また、土器の内側には焦げた炭化物が付着していたことから、この土器は煮炊きに使われていたことが分かっています。経験上、これは人類最古の調理の跡とされています。 　この遺跡は大平山元Ⅰ遺跡という縄文時代草創期の遺跡で、現在は国の史跡に指定されています。 　日本では、世界的に見てかなり早い段階で素焼きの土器に縄の模様をあしらった縄文土器が作られるようになり、それをもって縄文時代の始まりと定義していました。大平山元Ⅰ遺跡の土器には縄の模様はありませんでしたが、この土器の出現で、縄文時代の始まりが遡ったことになります。この時代の文化を縄文文化といいます。縄文時代は水田稲作が始まるころまで続きます。縄文土器は、黒褐色で、低温で焼き上げるため厚手でもろい特徴があり、火焔型土器のように美しく装飾されたものもあります（『日本美術図鑑・縄文時代』）。

（ロ）氷期の終焉と縄文海進p.27-28

🎧 Listen to the Audio
【課題】気候の変化によって人々の生活はどのように変化したのだろう。 今から約一万一〇〇〇年前、最後の氷期が終わり、世界は温暖な時代へと移行しました。温暖化により、植物の成長が早くなり、森林が増え、人々は木の実などの食料を確保しやすくなりました。また、氷が溶けて海面が上昇し、日本地域は大陸と分離されて再び日本列島が形成されました。 　気候の変化によって、日本列島は現在と同じような、四季のある温暖湿潤な気候になり、多種多様な植物が育つ環境になりました。また、海面が上昇したため、日本列島には多くの入江が形成されました。これを縄文海進といいます。入江には魚介類が棲みつきやすく、しかもそれらを獲りやすい地形ですから、縄文人は豊かな海の幸の恵みを受けるようになります。日本沿岸は暖流と寒流がぶつかるため、もともと豊かな漁場でしたが、縄文海進で森と海が接近したため、森の養分が直接入江に流れ込むようになり、さらに豊かな漁場となりました。 　温暖な時代の到来により、特に日本列島は、植物資源だけでなく、海産物も豊富に獲れるようになり、世界でも有数の豊かな土地になりました。現在でも日本食の文化は魚を中心としていますが、その起源は縄文時代にあったのです。 　この時代になると、日本列島から大型動物は姿を消し、猪や鹿などの機敏な動きをする中小の動物が増えました。それを捕らえるために鏃をつけた弓矢が用いられ、落とし穴も作られるようになりました。 　縄文時代の日本列島は豊かな環境に恵まれたため、人々は定住するようになりましたが、農耕や牧畜はあまり発達しませんでした。北部九州で稲作が始まるのは、縄文時代の晩期を待たなくてはなりません。 　縄文時代は約一万三〇〇〇年以上続きました。文化の芽生え（磨製石器の出現）を日本の歴史の始まりと考えるなら、日本の歴史の九割以上が先土器時代と縄文時代だったことになります。

注① 土器 粘土をこねて成型し、乾燥させ、焼き上げて作った容器。 注② 放射性炭素年代測定法 年代を測定する方法の1つ。大気や海水にはわずかな量の「炭素14」という放射性同位体が含まれている。炭素14は生物の体内にも取り込まれるが、生物が死に外界からの炭素14の供給が断たれると、炭素14は5,730年ごとに半減する性質を持つため、その存在量が経過時間とともに一定の割合で減少していく。したがって、動植物の遺物に含まれる炭素14を測定すれば、その生存年代を特定することができる。 注③ 1つの文化がこれほど長く続いたのは、世界の歴史で他に例がない。縄文時代は豊かで安定した時代だったため、新しい文化を取り入れる必要がなかったものと思われる。中国大陸で農耕が盛んになっても、日本列島の縄文人は、交流があるにもかかわらず、積極的に農耕を取り入れることはなかった。縄文文化は「農耕しない遅れた文化」なのではなく、自然と共生しながら採取経済を発展させた、成熟した文化だったといえる。 注④ 木の伐採と木材の加工 竪穴式住居などに用いる木材を伐採するには、硬い石で作った磨製石斧が用いられた。また、木材を加工するには、木材に細かい傷を付けて石斧で削る手法や、木材を水に濡らして、あるいは焦がして柔らかくしてから石斧で削る手法などが用いられた。

（ハ）縄文文化と縄文人の生活p.28-31

🎧 Listen to the Audio
【課題】縄文人の生活や信仰にはどのような特徴があったのだろう。 アメリカ人の動物学者エドワード・S・モースが、明治十年（一八七七）に鉄道で横浜から新橋に向かう途中、大森駅近くの崖に貝殻が積み重なっているのを発見し、後日、政府の許可を受けて発掘調査を行いました。それは縄文時代後期から末期ごろの貝塚でした。 　貝塚とは食べ残しやごみなどを捨てた遺跡で、貝殻、動物や魚の骨、土器の破片、土偶、石斧、石鏃、骨角器、人骨片などが発見されています。後に大森貝塚と呼ばれるようになります。このとき出土した土器に縄の模様が付いていたため、縄文土器と名づけられました。モースの発掘は日本の考古学の発祥とされています。大森貝塚は後に国の史跡に指定され、モースの発掘した遺物は、国の重要文化財に指定されています。 　全国の縄文時代の遺跡を調査した結果, 縄文人は打製石器、磨製石器、弓矢、骨角器などを使って狩猟、漁労、採集により食料を確保していたことが分かりました。骨角器とは、動物の骨格から作られた道具です。日本では鹿、猪、イルカなどの骨から作った釣針や銛などが、縄文時代の遺構から発見されています。人々は胡桃やドングリなどの木の実や山芋などの根菜を主食としていました。計画的に稗、栗、蕎麦、豆類、荏胡麻や瓢箪などの植物を栽培していたことも分かっています。土器が使われるようになると、煮る、蒸す、炊くといった調理法が加わっただけでなく、汁ものの調理が可能になり、木の実などのあく抜きもできるようになって、効率よく栄養を摂取できるようになりました。また、土器で飲飲食物を貯蔵することも可能になりました。酒を醸すようになったのもこのときです。 　人々は地面に掘った穴に柱を立てて、草で屋根を葺いた竪穴式住居に住み、集落を作るようになりました。丸太をくりぬいた丸木舟で沖に出て漁をし、遠方へも航海していました。青森県の三内丸山遺跡からは、大規模な集落の跡が発見されています。 　縄文人は、あらゆるものに精霊が宿ると考えていました。女性をかたどった土偶（土製の人形）や男性器を表現したと思われる石棒は、子孫繁栄を願う祭祀に用いられたと考えられています（『日本美術図鑑・縄文時代』）。自然との調和を重んじた縄文人の自然観は、現在の日本人の価値観にも色濃く残っています。また、縄文人の風習の一つに屈葬や抜歯があります。屈葬は、手足を折り曲げて葬ることで、死者の霊が災いを及ぼすのを防ぐため、また抜歯は、特定の永久歯を抜く習慣で、成人式の意味を持つものと考えられています。 　特別大きい住居が見られないこと、共同墓地が営まれたこと、震災や副葬品がないことなどから、縄文時代には人々の間に貧富の差や身分の上下はなかったと考えられています。 　日本列島に大規模な移住があったことは想定できないので、先土器時代人が縄文人の先祖で、また、骨格が現代日本人に似ていることから、縄文人は日本人の先祖と考えられています。

【コラム】縄文時代の大規模集落・三内丸山遺跡p.21

🎧 Listen to the Audio
三内丸山遺跡は, 青森県青森市にある縄文時代の大規模集落跡です。平成四年（一九九二）に発掘が始まりました。この遺跡の発掘により、これまで考えられていた縄文人像が大きく変化することになりました。 従来、縄文人は大きな集落を作らず、食物の栽培はしていなかったと考えられていました。ところが、三内丸山の縄文人は、栗の木を計画的に植林し、下草を刈るなどして、念入りに管理していたことが分かったのです。また、直径と深さがともに二メートルの六つの巨大な柱の跡が発見されたことから、かなり大規模な建物が存在し、高度な土木建築技術があったことを思わせます。 三内丸山遺跡は、縄文時代前期から中期にかけ、五九〇〇年前ごろから四三〇〇年前ごろまで、およそ一六〇〇年間営まれました。江戸（東京）は徳川家康が城を築いてから約四〇〇年、京都は桓武天皇が遷都してから約一二〇〇年ですから、それと比べると、太古の昔に一六〇〇年もの間集落が営まれたのは、すごいことです。住居の跡は三〇〇棟以上にのぼると推定されていて、同時期に三〇〇〇棟以上の住居が存在していたわけではないにせよ、その規模は、これまでの縄文遺跡の常識を破るものでした。 遠方との交易も盛んだったようです。糸魚川の翡翠、岩手の琥珀、秋田のアスファルトなどが出土しています。また、遺跡には大規模な墓地が造営され、故人を丁重に葬る文化があったことが分かります。 三内丸山遺跡の発掘の結果、縄文人は採取経済を基礎とする社会としては、稀に見る高度な社会を構築していたことが分かりました。

（二）世界の文明と日本p.31-33

🎧 Listen to the Audio
【課題】世界の文明はどこで起こり、どのような特徴があるのだろう。日本と世界の文明にはどのような共通点があるのだろう。また、お互いにどのように影響し合いながら形成されていったのだろう。 日本で出土した世界最古級の土器以外にも、同じような古い時期の土器として、ロシアと中国の例があります。ロシアのグロマトゥーハ遺跡で発見された土器は約一万五〇〇0年前のものです。また、中国の湖南省の洞窟で発見された土器が約一万八〇〇〇年前という報告もあります。いずれにしても、東アジアに最古級の土器が集中しているといえます。ところが、南アジア、西アジア、アフリカ、ヨーロッパなどでは、日本に八〇〇〇年ほど遅れて土器を作るようになりました。少なくとも、土器に関しては、日本を含む東アジアが極端に古いといえます。 　ところで、中国湖南省の約一万八〇〇〇年前という土器は、日本の考古学者が調査を要請しても拒否され、しかも土器自体を調べたのではない、土器の上下の地層の動物骨などから割り出したもので、約三〇〇〇年間の開きがあり、上限が一万八〇〇〇年ということでしかありません。その後、中国は江西省で約二万年前の土器が出土したと発表しましたが、付着物を測定せず地層から割り出した、信憑性の低いものでした。 　農耕を開始して、都市を形成し、文字を用いて、広範囲な交易をしていることなどをもって文明の成立と考えられてきました。どの文明が世界の文明の祖であるかは研究の途上にあり、まだ定説はありません。 　かつて、エジプト文明、メソポタミア文明、インダス文明、中国文明を世界四大文明と呼び、世界中の文明はすべてこれら四大文明の亜流であると考えられていた時期がありました。しかし、考古学の研究が進むと、同じ条件を満たす例が各地に発見され、四大文明の技術が世界の文明の祖であるという考え方は、現在では否定されています。 　日本列島では本格的な食料生産と都市形成の時期は遅れましたが、日本列島から最古級の磨製石器と最古級の土器が発見されていて、日本は独自の文化の起源を持っているといえます。アメリカを代表する国際政治学者であるサミュエル・ハンチントンは著書『文明の衝突』で、ほとんどの学者は日本を固有の文明と認識しているとして、五世紀以降の日本文化を「日本文明」と呼びました。日本列島は地理的に大陸から隔てられていたため、他の地域とは異なった特色ある文化を発展させていきますが、日本文化は他地域の文明と無関係に発展・形成されたわけではありません。日本列島の人々は、大陸や朝鮮半島から積極的に文化を受け容れ発展させ、また東アジアの多くの地域に文化的影響を与えました。日本文化は、特に中国の文明と相互に強い影響を与えながら発展していくことになります。 　エジプト、メソポタミア、インダス、黄河・長江、メソアメリカ、アンデスをはじめとする地域では、日本列島より早い段階で農耕と牧畜が始まりました。大河の周辺地域では穀類中心、また山岳地帯のアンデスや、島嶼のパプアニューギニアではジャガイモなどの根菜類中心の農耕が行われました。 　大陸では、世界的にも特に早い時期に農耕が始まっています。約一万二〇〇〇年前に、長江（揚子江）中流域で稲作を中心とした農耕が始まっていたことが確認されています。また、水田としては, 長江中下流域の約六四〇〇年前の水田の跡が最も古く、長江流域が稲作の起源であると考えられています。 　その他、最古級の農耕としては、シリアのテル・アブ・フレイラ遺跡に約一万一〇〇〇年前のライムギの農耕の跡が、また、パプアニューギニアに約九〇〇〇年前の芋類を作るための灌漑施設の跡があります。 　日本列島は豊かな温帯林に包まれ、周囲を海に囲まれた環境にあり、山海の幸に恵までした。このように、他の地域に比べて食料を得ることが容易だったため、日本では本格的な農耕を開始する時期が遅れたと考えられています。ところが、縄文時代晩期になると寒冷化により自然の実りが乏しくなりました。このような時期に縄文人も重い腰を上げて稲作に着手することになったと考えられています。食料事情が極度悪化すると幼い動植物まで捕獲するようになりますが、縄文時代晩期の貝塚に幼獣の個体が増える形跡はなく、また、そのころの縄文人の骨や歯に、成長が止まるような障害はほとんど観察されていません。縄文人は自然環境が変化するなか、余裕を持って農耕社会に移行していったことが分かります。その後、日本列島の人々が積極的に大陸の文化を取り込んでいくことは、「弥生時代」以降で詳しく述べます。

(A) Japan’s Jomon Pottery is Among the Oldest in the Worldp.26-27

🎧 Listen to the Audio
【Question】 When did people on the Japanese archipelago begin using pottery? In Japan, the beginning of the Jomon period is defined by the inception of pottery making, while the start of the Yayoi period (which marks the end of the Jomon period) is defined by the beginning of full-scale wet-rice cultivation. In 1998 (Heisei 10), during the reconstruction of a private home in Sotogahama Town, Aomori Prefecture, an excavation survey conducted by the former Kanita Town Board of Education uncovered pottery that would significantly rewrite the history of world archaeology. The discovered pottery consisted entirely of small fragments and bore no patterns. However, radiocarbon dating of organic charred remains adhering to the fragments revealed that the pottery was approximately 16,300 years old, making it among the oldest in the world. This measurement pushed the estimated emergence of pottery in Japan back by about 4,000 years compared to what had previously been believed. Following this, numerous ancient pottery pieces were confirmed across a wide area of the Japanese archipelago. For instance, pottery from the Fukui Cave in Nagasaki Prefecture is estimated to be about 16,000 years old; from the Gotenyama Site in Tokyo, about 16,300 years old; from the Miyagase-Kitahara Site in Kanagawa Prefecture, about 15,500 years old; and from the Taisho 3 Site in Hokkaido, about 14,500 years old. The fact that the Fukui Cave and Gotenyama sites are from almost the exact same period as the Sotogahama site indicates that pottery technology spread from Hokkaido in the north to Kyushu in the west within a remarkably short span of time. Stone arrowheads discovered at this site are also considered the oldest in the world. Furthermore, because charred soot was found on the interior of the pottery, it is clear that these vessels were used for boiling and cooking. Consequently, this is regarded as the oldest evidence of human cooking. This location, known as the Odai Yamamoto I Site, dates back to the Incipient Jomon period and is currently designated as a National Historic Site. Previously, the Jomon period was defined by the appearance of earthenware adorned with cord-marked patterns ("Jomon"), which developed at a considerably early stage globally. Although the pottery from the Odai Yamamoto I Site lacked these cord markings, its discovery pushed back the chronological beginning of the Jomon period. The culture of this era is called Jomon culture, and the period lasted until the introduction of wet-rice cultivation. Jomon pottery is dark brown, thick, and brittle due to being fired at low temperatures. Some vessels, such as "flame-style" pottery, feature exceptionally beautiful and elaborate decorations.

(B) The End of the Ice Age and the Jomon Transgressionp.27-28

【Question】 How did people’s lives change as a result of climatic shifts? With the arrival of this warm era, the Japanese archipelago became one of the most resource-rich lands in the world, yielding an abundance of both plant resources and seafood. Even today, Japanese culinary culture revolves heavily around fish, and the origins of this tradition lie in the Jomon period. If we consider the dawn of culture—marked by the appearance of polished stone tools—as the beginning of Japanese history, it means that the Paleolithic and Jomon periods combined account for over 90% of Japan's entire historical timeline.

(C) Jomon Culture and the Lifestyle of the Jomon Peoplep.28-31

🎧 Listen to the Audio
【Question】 What were the characteristics of the lifestyle and spiritual beliefs of the Jomon people? American science scholar Edward S. Morse was traveling by train from Yokohama to Shinbashi in 1877 (Meiji 10) when he noticed a massive accumulation of seashells exposed on a cliff near Omori Station. He later obtained government permission to conduct an excavation. This site turned out to be a shell midden (mound) dating from the Late to Final Jomon period. Shell middens are archaeological sites where ancient people discarded food remains and refuse. They yield seashells, animal and fish bones, pottery fragments, clay figurines (dogu), stone axes, stone arrowheads, bone and antler tools, and human skeletal remains. The site Morse found came to be known as the Omori Shell Midden. Because the pottery unearthed here featured cord-like impressions, it was named "Jomon" (cord-marked) pottery. Morse's excavation is recognized as the birth of Japanese archaeology. The Omori Shell Midden was later designated a National Historic Site, and the artifacts excavated by Morse are designated as Important Cultural Properties. Surveys of Jomon sites nationwide have revealed that the Jomon people secured their food through hunting, fishing, and gathering using chipped stone tools, polished stone tools, bows and arrows, and bone and antler implements. Bone and antler implements refer to tools crafted from animal skeletons; fishhooks and harpoons made from the bones of deer, wild boars, and dolphins have been found in Jomon sites across Japan. People relied on walnuts and acorns, as well as root vegetables like wild yams, as their dietary staples. Evidence shows they also systematically cultivated plants such as barnyard millet, chestnuts, buckwheat, beans, perilla, and bottle gourds. The introduction of pottery expanded their culinary repertoire to include boiling, steaming, and stewing. It also enabled them to cook soups and leach tannins from bitter acorns, allowing for more efficient nutrient absorption. Furthermore, pottery made it possible to store food and beverages, and it was during this time that the brewing of alcohol began. People lived in pit-dwellings constructed by digging a shallow pit into the ground, erecting wooden pillars, and thatching the roof with grass, forming organized settlements. They also ventured out to sea in dugout canoes carved from large logs to fish and engage in long-distance maritime travel. At the Sannai-Maruyama Site in Aomori Prefecture, evidence of a massive, long-term settlement has been discovered. The Jomon people practiced animism, believing that spirits resided in all elements of nature. Female-shaped clay figurines (Dogu) and phallic stone rods (sekibou) are believed to have been used in rituals to pray for fertility and the prosperity of descendants. This worldview, which emphasizes harmony with nature, remains deeply embedded in the values of modern Japanese people. The Jomon people also practiced specific customs such as flexed burials (burying the deceased with their arms and legs bent) and ritual tooth extraction. Flexed burials are thought to have been performed to prevent the spirits of the dead from returning to cause harm, while tooth extraction—the removal of specific permanent teeth—is believed to have served as a rite of passage into adulthood. The absence of exceptionally large dwellings, the presence of communal burial grounds, and the general lack of lavish grave goods indicate that there was no significant wealth disparity or social hierarchy among the Jomon people. Since there is no evidence of large-scale migration into the archipelago during this transition, it is deduced that the Paleolithic people were the direct ancestors of the Jomon people. Furthermore, because their skeletal structures closely resemble those of modern Japanese people, the Jomon people are considered the foundational ancestors of the Japanese population.

【Column】 A Large-Scale Jomon Settlement: The Sannai-Maruyama Sitep.21

🎧 Listen to the Audio
The Sannai-Maruyama Site, located in Aomori City, Aomori Prefecture, is a massive settlement site from the Jomon period. Excavations began in 1992 (Heisei 4). The discoveries made at this site fundamentally transformed the conventional historical understanding of the Jomon people. Previously, it was widely believed that the Jomon people did not form large settlements and did not cultivate plants. However, evidence at Sannai-Maruyama revealed that the inhabitants systematically planted chestnut trees, cleared undergrowth, and carefully managed their arboreal resources. Furthermore, the discovery of six enormous pillar holes, each measuring two meters in diameter and depth, indicates the existence of a monumental structure requiring highly sophisticated civil engineering and architectural skills. The Sannai-Maruyama settlement was occupied from the Early to Middle Jomon period, roughly from 5,900 to 4,300 years ago, spanning a period of about 1,600 years. To put this into perspective, Edo (Tokyo) has existed for about 400 years since Tokugawa Ieyasu built his castle, and Kyoto has existed for about 1,200 years since Emperor Kanmu relocated the capital. A continuous settlement lasting 1,600 years in antiquity is extraordinary. The total number of dwellings at the site is estimated to exceed 3,000. While these houses did not all exist simultaneously, the sheer scale completely shattered previous assumptions about Jomon settlements. Long-distance trade was also highly active. Artifacts discovered include jade from Itoigawa, amber from Iwate, and asphalt from Akita. Additionally, the site features a large, organized cemetery, demonstrating a culture that treated the deceased with great care and reverence. The excavations at the Sannai-Maruyama Site proved that the Jomon people constructed a remarkably sophisticated society, achieving a level of complexity rarely seen among cultures based fundamentally on a foraging economy.

(D) Global Civilizations and Japanp.31-33

🎧 Listen to the Audio
【Question】 Where did world civilizations emerge, and what were their characteristics? What commonalities exist between Japanese culture and global civilizations? How did they influence each other during their formation? In addition to the world's oldest class of pottery found in Japan, similarly ancient pottery examples exist in Russia and China. Pottery discovered at the Gromatukha site in Russia dates back approximately 15,000 years, and there are reports of pottery found in a cave in Hunan Province, China, dating to around 18,000 years ago. This indicates that the world's earliest pottery technology was concentrated in East Asia. In contrast, populations in South Asia, West Asia, Africa, and Europe did not begin producing pottery until roughly 8,000 years later. In terms of ceramic technology, East Asia was exceptionally advanced. However, regarding the 18,000-year-old pottery from Hunan Province, requests by Japanese archaeologists to inspect the site were denied. Furthermore, the dating was not conducted on the pottery itself but was inferred from animal bones found in the surrounding soil layers, leaving a margin of error of about 3,000 years with 18,000 years as merely an upper limit. China subsequently announced the discovery of 20,000-year-old pottery in Jiangxi Province, but this too was deduced from soil layers without direct testing of organic components, making its reliability questionable. Traditionally, the establishment of a civilization has been defined by the onset of agriculture, urban formation, the utilization of writing systems, and extensive trade networks. Determining which civilization stands as the ultimate ancestor of global society remains an ongoing area of research, and no definitive consensus exists. At one time, Egypt, Mesopotamia, the Indus, and China were collectively referred to as the "Four Great Ancient Civilizations," and it was believed that all global civilizations were offshoots of these four. However, as archaeological research advanced, sites meeting the criteria for civilization were discovered across various other regions, and the theory that all world civilizations originated exclusively from these four centers has since been discredited. Although full-scale food production and urbanization occurred later on the Japanese archipelago, the discovery of some of the world's oldest polished stone tools and ceramics indicates that Japan possesses an independent cultural origin. In his book The Clash of Civilizations, the prominent American political scientist Samuel Huntington noted that most scholars recognize Japan as a distinct, self-contained civilization, referring to Japanese culture from the 5th century onward as "Japanese Civilization". Because the Japanese archipelago was geographically separated from the Asian continent, it developed a unique culture with distinct characteristics. However, Japanese culture did not evolve in total isolation. The people of the Japanese archipelago actively embraced and adapted cultural elements from the mainland and the Korean Peninsula, while also exerting cultural influence over East Asia. Japanese culture developed through a dynamic history of mutual influence, particularly with Chinese civilization. Agriculture and pastoralism began earlier than in Japan in regions such as Egypt, Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley, the Yellow Rarer, Yangtze Rivers, Mesoamerica, and the Andes. Civilizations along major river valleys focused primarily on cereal crops, whereas mountainous regions like the Andes and island regions like Papua New Guinea develop agriculture centered on root vegetables, such as potatoes and yams. On the Eurasian continent, agriculture began exceptionally early. It has been confirmed fledged that rice cultivation was established in the middle reaches of the Yangtze River approximately 12,000 years ago. The oldest physical remnants of rice paddies, dating back about 6,400 years, were found in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze, confirming this region as the cradle of rice agriculture. Other ancient agricultural milestones include evidence of rye cultivation at the Tell Abu Hureyra site in Syria around 11,000 years ago, and irrigation infrastructure for tuber cultivation in Papua New Guinea dating to roughly 9,000 years ago. The Japanese archipelago was covered with lush temperate forests and surrounded by rich oceans, providing an abundance of both terrestrial and marine food sources. Because procuring food was relatively easy compared to other regions, the transition to full-scale agricultural reliance was delayed in Japan. However, during the Final Jomon period, global cooling reduced natural foraging yields. Facing this environmental stress, the Jomon people finally committed to adopting rice cultivation. When food security drops drastically, societies typically over-exploit resources, resulting in high proportions of juvenile animals in refuse heaps. Yet, Jomon shell middens from this transitional phase show no increase in juvenile animal remains, and skeletal examinations of Jomon people from this period reveal almost no signs of arrested physical growth caused by starvation. This demonstrates that the Jomon people transitioned into an agricultural society deliberately and with a degree of socioeconomic stability. The active integration of mainland culture following this shift will be detailed in the "Yayoi Period" section.

(E) The State of the Eurasian Continentp.23-24

🎧 Listen to the Audio
【Question】 What are the defining features of global civilizations? What elements of these civilizations continue to influence modern Japanese culture? Once human societies established stable food production through agriculture, they settled permanently, constructed urban centers, built monumental temples, and eventually formed ancient states. This led to wealth disparities and the emergence of monarchs to govern the populace. Bronze implements (a durable alloy of copper and tin) were crafted for rituals and warfare, and writing systems emerged during this pivotal era. Consequently, states began to undergo cycles of rise and fall. In Egypt, agriculture flourished because the Nile River flooded annually, depositing nutrient-rich silt across the river valley. Because these annual floods erased property boundaries, sophisticated surveying techniques were developed. To predict the timing of the floods, astronomy emerged, leading to the creation of a decimal system and a solar calendar with a 365-day year. The Egyptians also utilized a hieroglyphic writing system (Holy Characters). Around 3100 BCE, a unified kingdom was established, and the pharaohs wielded absolute authority, commanding the construction of massive pyramids and temples. In Mesopotamia (modern-day Iraq), situated between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, agriculture thrived due to the exceptionally fertile soil. Around 3000 BCE, several independent city-states emerged. These walled urban centers contained grand temples and royal palaces. The Mesopotamians recorded information using cuneiform script incised onto clay tablets. The combination of Egyptian hieroglyphs and Mesopotamian cuneiform would eventually evolve into the modern alphabet. Additionally, the Mesopotamians developed a lunar calendar based on the phases of the moon, created the seven-day week system, and invented the sexagesimal (base-60) system for measuring time. In the 18th century BCE, King Hammurabi unified Mesopotamia and promulgated the Code of Hammurabi. In the Indus River Valley, flowing through northwestern India, an urban-centric civilization epitomized by cities like Mohenjo-daro flourished around 2500 BCE. These cities were characterized by highly organized grid-pattern streets and advanced underground drainage networks. They utilized the Indus script. Around 1500 BCE, Indo-Aryans migrated into the region from Central Asia, establishing kingdoms governed by a rigid social hierarchy topped by priests known as Brahmins. This system laid the foundation for the caste system that persists in modern India. In China, home to the Yellow and Yangtze rivers, agricultural cultures emerged around 6000 BCE. A wet-rice cultivation culture developed along the southern Yangtze River valley, while a farming culture focused on millet and broomcorn millet arose along the northern Yellow River basin. Wet-rice cultivation can be sustained continuously without fertilizer and yields a high caloric return per unit of land. Conversely, millet farming requires active fertilization and is prone to consecutive cultivation injury, resulting in lower yields that necessitate highly labor-intensive farming methods. Consequently, wealth disparities grew more rapidly within the Yellow River culture, leading powerful factions to forcefully annex neighboring regions. This dynamic spurred the rise of stratified societies and institutionalized states. Around the 17th century BCE, the Shang (Yin) Dynasty arose along the Yellow River basin, utilizing advanced bronze vessels for state rituals and developing oracle bone script, the oldest known form of Chinese writing. Around 1100 BCE, the Zhou Dynasty overthrew the Shang. As Zhou authority eventually waned, the region fractured into a multitude of competing factions, entering the tumultuous Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods by the 8th century BCE. During this era, iron implements emerged, transforming both military warfare and agricultural productivity. Meanwhile, on the Korean Peninsula, the production of polished stone tools and pottery began later than in Japan, and the inhabitants led a lifestyle centered on hunting, fishing, and gathering, similar to the Jomon people.

(F) The Dawn of Religionp.24

🎧 Listen to the Audio
【Question】 How did global religions originate and spread? Consider their relationships with ancient civilizations. In antiquity, human beings felt overwhelmed by the immense power of natural phenomena, leading them to conceptualize "gods" as entities transcending human capability. People began praying to these deities for health, favorable weather, and protection. Over time, spiritual leaders emerged to interpret divine will, leading to the formation of distinct, localized religions. Certain faiths eventually transitioned their geographical origins to become global phenomena. Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam are recognized as the world's three major religions. In the 5th century BCE, Siddhartha Gautama, born a prince of the Shakya clan in India, attained enlightenment through rigorous asceticism and meditation. He formulated a doctrine to liberate humanity from worldly desires, marking the birth of Buddhism. Buddhism spread across vast expanses of Asia, arriving in Japan in the 6th century, where it underwent a unique historical evolution. In the Levant (Palestine region) of West Asia, the monotheistic faith of Judaism emerged, its core tenets codified within the Hebrew Bible (Old Testament). Around the turn of the common era, Jesus introduced a revolutionary interpretation of Jewish law. Although many recognized him as the Messiah, he was executed by the Roman authorities. Following his death, his disciples propagated his teachings, establishing Christianity. The core doctrine—that humanity is born inherent with sin yet remains eligible for unconditional divine love—is preserved within the New Testament. Christianity spread throughout Europe and eventually worldwide. It was introduced to Japan in the 16th century. In the 7th century, on the Arabian Peninsula, Muhammad founded Islam after receiving revelations from the supreme deity, Allah, building upon the foundations of Judaism and Christianity. Islam commands absolute submission to divine will and strictly prohibits idolatry. Its sacred text, the Quran, serves as the comprehensive legal and moral framework for Islamic society. Islam spread rapidly from the Arabian Peninsula across North Africa, the Middle East, and into Southeast Asia. Meanwhile, the inhabitants of the Japanese archipelago maintained an animistic belief system, holding that divine spirits resided within natural elements like the sun, mountains, and oceans. They venerated both these natural deities and their ancestral lineages, a spiritual tradition known as Shinto.

(G) Tansen Korin: The Descent of the Heavenly Grandsonp.25

🎧 Listen to the Audio
【Question】 How do the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki chronicle the lineage of the imperial ancestors? While the Kojiki does not detail the physical origins of the human race or the Japanese population, it records the ancestral lineage of the Emperors as follows: Following the creation of the Japanese archipelago, the deity Izanagi performed a ritual purification. When he washed his left eye, Amaterasu-Omikami (The Sun Goddess) was born; when he washed his right eye, Tsukuyomi-no-Mikoto (The Moon God) appeared; and when he washed his nose, Susanoo-no-Mikoto emerged. These noble deities are collectively referred to as the Sankishi (Three Noble Children). Amaterasu-Omikami was commanded by her father to govern Takamagahara (The High Plain of Heaven). Meanwhile, in Ashihara-no-Nakatsukuni (The Central Land of Reed Plains, representing the terrestrial world), Susanoo's descendant, Okuninushi-no-Kami, successfully developed a prosperous earthly kingdom. However, following diplomatic negotiations, it was agreed that sovereignty over the terrestrial world would be ceded to the lineage of Amaterasu-Omikami, a narrative known as the Myth of the Transfer of the Land (Kuniyuzuri). Amaterasu-Omikami then dispatched her grandson to govern the earthly realm. The deity who descended to earth under this divine mandate was Ninigi-no-Mikoto, the direct ancestor of Kamuyamato-Iwarebiko-no-Mikoto (the legendary first Emperor, Jimmu). Because the grandson of the supreme heavenly deity descended to the earthly realm, this event is called Tansen Korin (The Descent of the Heavenly Grandson). Upon his departure, Amaterasu-Omikami bestowed three sacred treasures upon Ninigi-no-Mikoto: the Yasakani-no-Magatama (comma-shaped jewel), the Yata-no-Kagami (sacred mirror), and the Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi (sacred sword). These constitute the Three Sacred Treasures, the ultimate symbols of imperial legitimacy. They have been transmitted through generations of successive Emperors down to the modern day. The Nihon Shoki preserves a similar narrative, noting that Amaterasu-Omikami explicitly commanded her grandson to take the sacred rice cultivated within the heavenly fields of Takamagahara down to earth. The myth of Tansen Korin serves a dual purpose: it establishes the divine origin of the imperial lineage and asserts that the origin of agriculture—the socioeconomic bedrock of ancient Japanese society—was a divine gift brought to earth by the imperial ancestors. This narrative offers invaluable insight into the spiritual worldview and values held by the early Japanese people.
